Maximum Probability Theorem

IEEE Transactions on Artificial Intelligence, 2021. With Ehsan Emad Marvasti, Ulas Bagci and Hassan Foroosh.

A model is not a point in parameter space. A model is an event, a set in the probability space, and like every other event it has a probability. Not a density. A probability mass, no matter how you parameterize it.

That sentence is the whole theorem in disguise. For years the question that kept my head steaming was: what is the prior over the parameters of a neural network? Everyone puts a Gaussian on the weights and calls it a day. But look, when the model is complex, the norm of the parameter vector doesn’t really say anything about how the model behaves. Two networks with very different weights can do the exact same thing. I wanted a prior that cares about behaviour, not about the coordinates we happened to pick.

MPT turns the problem upside down. You don’t put a prior on the parameters at all. You only need a prior on the things you can actually observe, the input and the output. Then the theorem tells you the probability of the model itself.

Theory · The Maximum Probability Theorem

Take a probability space (Ω, Σ, P), a random variable Z with a finite range, and any event M ∈ Σ with conditional distribution P(z | M). Then

P(M) ≤ minz P(z)P(z | M)

The bound equals 1 only when P(z | M) = P(z) everywhere, so −log P(M) measures how far the model has walked away from the prior: its information content. For training, the minimum is replaced by the softmin family, which is a lower bound for every α > 0 and becomes the minimum as α → ∞:

Pα(M) = ( Σz (P(z)P(z | M))−α )−1/α
Theorem 1 · ProofThe Maximum Probability TheoremShow the proof

For any event σ and any outcome v of V, the law of total probability splits P(v) over σ and its complement σ̄:

P(v) = P(v | σ) P(σ) + P(v | σ̄) P(σ̄)

The second term is never negative, so P(v) ≥ P(v | σ) P(σ). Wherever P(v | σ) ≠ 0 we can divide:

P(σ) ≤ P(v)P(v | σ)

This holds for every v, so it holds for the smallest ratio, and P(σ) ≤ infv P(v) / P(v | σ). ∎

Short and simple, and yet it is the whole foundation: every conditional distribution on an observable is an event with a bounded, computable probability.

Theorem 2 · ProofLooking closer can only lower the boundShow the proof

Statement. If a random variable W extends V, meaning every preimage W−1(w) lies inside some V−1(v), then 𝒫(σ ≻ W) ≤ 𝒫(σ ≻ V). In particular, observing more variables, H = (V, Z), never raises the probability of a model.

Proof. Let Rv(W) be the outcomes of W that lie inside V−1(v). Then P(v) = Σw ∈ Rv P(w) and P(v | σ) = Σw ∈ Rv P(w | σ). By Theorem 1 applied to W, P(w) ≥ P(w | σ) 𝒫(σ ≻ W) for every w. Summing over Rv(W):

P(v) ≥ P(v | σ) · 𝒫(σ ≻ W)

so every ratio P(v) / P(v | σ) is at least 𝒫(σ ≻ W), and so is their infimum, 𝒫(σ ≻ V). ∎

The more finely you look at a model, the more specified it becomes, and the less probable. The bound is relative to what you observe.

Proposition · ProofThe softmin is a lower bound, and tight as α → ∞Show the proof

Write rv = P(v) / P(v | σ) and let rmin be the smallest. For α > 0 every term of the sum is positive, and one of them is rmin−α, so

Σv rv−α ≥ rmin−α   ⟹   Pα(σ) = (Σv rv−α)−1/α ≤ rmin

which is the bound of Theorem 1. On the other side, the sum is at most |R(V)| rmin−α, so Pα(σ) ≥ |R(V)|−1/α rmin, and |R(V)|−1/α → 1 as α → ∞. ∎

Read the theorem slowly. The ratio compares what the world believes about z, the prior, with what the model believes. The moment the model becomes very sure about one particular state, that state’s ratio gets small, and the model as a whole becomes improbable. So a confident, peaky model is an expensive model.

The most probable coin

Take the simplest observable there is, a coin, with a uniform prior on heads and tails. A model is a coin with bias θ. MPT gives its probability as min(½ / θ, ½ / (1 − θ)). The fair coin has probability 1. A coin that always lands heads has probability ½, not zero, because it still overlaps with half of what the prior believes. And two coins with biases 0.8 and 0.9 are not disjoint events, they mostly say the same thing.

Theory · The objective is a set operation

The objective is the probability that the model and the oracle happen together. Assuming the model and the oracle are independent given Z, it splits into the familiar likelihood and the probability of the model:

ln P(Mθ, M*) = ln P(M* | Mθ) + ln P(Mθ)
≥ Σz P(z | M*) ln P(z | Mθ) + ln Pα(Mθ)

The first term is the cross entropy everyone already uses. The second is the regularizer. Nobody had to design it, it falls out of Kolmogorov’s axioms, it depends only on how the model behaves, and so it is invariant to reparameterization.

DerivationFrom the intersection to cross entropy plus the regularizerShow the proof

Write the intersection in its conditional form, ln P(Mθ, M*) = ln P(M* | Mθ) + ln P(Mθ), and marginalize the first term over the observable z:

ln Σz P(M* | z, Mθ) P(z | Mθ)

If the model and the oracle are independent given z, then P(M* | z, Mθ) = P(z | M*) P(M*) / P(z). With a uniform prior on z, P(z) and P(M*) are constants and the objective is ln Σz P(z | M*) P(z | Mθ) + ln P(Mθ). Replace P(Mθ) by its softmin lower bound Pα, and use Jensen’s inequality on the concave logarithm:

ln Σz P(z | M*) P(z | Mθ) ≥ Σz P(z | M*) ln P(z | Mθ)

The right hand side is the cross entropy. So intersection ≥ cross entropy + ln Pα(Mθ), and at α = 1 the regularizer is constant. ∎

That is what I mean by black box regularization: it comes in as one more term in the objective function, and it works on any model whose likelihood you can compute.

Theory · Forgetting is regularization

MPT is a theory of forgetting.

The information content of a model is −log P(M): how many bits the model believes beyond what the prior already believed, everything it has committed to memory. Maximizing P(M) removes that information. The model is asked to forget every bit it does not need to explain the data.

That is what regularization always was. A Gaussian prior on the weights is a clumsy way of asking a network to forget, written in the coordinates of its parameters. MPT asks for the forgetting directly, in terms of what the model does. Even smoothness is forgetting: 4.2432 is a region because we forgot the digits after it.

I(M) = −log P(M)   ·   maximize P(M) = forget

Does it work on a real network?

We trained a vanilla CNN with the intersection objective and different values of α. With α = 1, which is plain cross entropy, the test loss goes down and then climbs back up, the usual overfitting. With larger α the test loss settles and stays there. No weight decay, no hand tuned prior on the weights.

1080 networks, and the result that held every time

One network proves nothing; anyone can find a lucky seed. So in the triangle paper we asked a harder question: if you don’t tune anything, if you train like someone who just got a new dataset, does MPT still help? We trained a crowd of networks and looked at the whole crowd.

How we measured it. For each value of α we took every network trained with it, across all the batch sizes, seeds and objectives, and computed two numbers from their test accuracy: the mean, how well the crowd generalizes on average, and the variance, how much it depends on luck. A network that diverged counts as zero accuracy, so instability is not hidden; it pulls the mean down and the variance up. In the plots the line is the mean and the shaded band is the spread.

The empirical finding

As α grows, the mean test accuracy goes up and its variance goes down. On CIFAR10, on CIFAR100, on MNIST. Every time.

Not on one lucky network, on 1080 networks trained blind. More MPT, better generalization on average, and much less of a lottery. Robustness to noise in the input rose with α the same way, and out of distribution detection improved with it. Seriously: MPT works, and it works consistently.

From the triangle paper: test accuracy against α, the line is the mean over all networks trained with that α and the band is the spread. Read each plot from left to right: up, and narrower. The tab has the rest of the study.

Where it came from: in 2015 I was playing with rejection sampling and wrote a report on the distribution of the rejected samples. Years later, stuck on an optimization problem, I realized that the acceptance rate is exactly this bound, the probability of the model. The tab shows it moving.

Probabilistic Optimization

Derivative free optimization with probabilistic neighbourhoods. A working notebook, and a draft.

Calculus is built on infinitesimals. A derivative asks how f changes in a neighbourhood of x, as the neighbourhood shrinks to nothing. What if the neighbourhood was a probability distribution instead, and never shrank to nothing?

Imagine you want the minimum of a function that has no useful derivative. A staircase, for example: flat everywhere, with jumps. The gradient is zero on every step, and gradient descent just sits there. Or a function with noise on it, bounded, but with a first derivative that jumps around without limit. These are not exotic. They happen in practice all the time, every time something is quantized, counted or measured.

So look at the function through a distribution. Sample around x, evaluate, and let the samples tell you which way is down. The learning rate is not a separate knob anymore, it is embedded in the variance of the distribution. And the distribution itself is a model, so MPT can regularize it: it keeps the neighbourhood from collapsing too early onto one point.

The open problem

How do we define the gradient in a neighbourhood, instead of in the one special neighbourhood called the infinitesimal?

The derivative is built on variations of a function in a small neighbourhood, and calculus lets that neighbourhood shrink to a point. Let the neighbourhood be any probability distribution instead and the size of the variation is easy, the variance of f(x) over the neighbourhood tells you how much f changes. But a gradient is also a direction, and for a distributional neighbourhood we have no settled definition of it. A first, naive candidate:

∇ = ∫ (x − E[x]) · sign(f(x) − E[f]) · p(x) dx

It works on the toys below, and the learning rate quietly disappears into the variance of p. The algorithm in the theory box is a better answer, derived from MPT. Whether there is a definition as clean as the limit in calculus is, for me, one of the most important open questions in optimization.

Theory · The objective

Let P(x | Mθ) be the sampling distribution, and β < 0 when we want to minimize f. The objective, generalized with the Maximum Probability Theorem, is

g(θ; α) = ln Σx P(x | Mθ) eβf(x) − 1α ln Σx P(x | Mθ)α

With α = 1 the second term vanishes and this is the plain smoothed objective. Its gradient is a difference of two distributions over the samples, both pushed through the score ∇θ ln P(x | Mθ).

The algorithm

  1. Draw N samples x1 … xN from P(x | Mθ) and keep their log probabilities.
  2. Evaluate β f(xi) for each sample.
  3. Compute two weights per sample:
    Li = eβf(xi)Σj eβf(xj) Ri = P(xi | Mθ)αΣj P(xj | Mθ)α Gi = Li − Ri
  4. Step θ along Σi Gi ∇θ ln P(xi | Mθ). The log probability is a deterministic function of θ, so any autograd framework does this part.

L pulls the distribution toward the samples that did well. R pushes back against the samples the distribution already loves too much, which is MPT suppressing the most probable states again. The estimate is biased, it is importance sampling; rejection sampling would make it unbiased, at a price.

DerivationThe gradient of the objectiveShow the proof

For the first term, the chain rule through ln P(x | Mθ) gives a weighted average of the score:

∇θ ln Σx P(x | Mθ) eβf(x) = Σx P(x | Mθ) eβf(x)Σx′ P(x′ | Mθ) eβf(x′) ∇θ ln P(x | Mθ)

and for the MPT term, since ∇Pα = α Pα ∇ ln P,

∇θ 1α ln Σx P(x | Mθ)α = Σx P(x | Mθ)αΣx′ P(x′ | Mθ)α ∇θ ln P(x | Mθ)

Estimate both sums with the same N samples from P(x | Mθ), dividing out the sampling probability, and the weights become exactly Li and Ri of the algorithm. Both are probability vectors over the samples, so their difference G sums to zero. ∎

Another view

In machine learning you can put the uncertainty on the observations instead of the parameters. Noise in the observations turns into a much more complicated noise in parameter space, and you never have to pretend the parameters are random variables. Write the model as an energy based model, P(x | Mθ) ∝ ef(x; θ) P(x), and the MPT bound becomes the ratio of the average of ef to its maximum. Maximizing it forces f to be smooth. That is where energy based models, the Maximum Probability Theorem and probabilistic optimization become one framework.

Theory · How much prior to mix in

A single degenerate observation x0, mixed with a uniform prior in proportion q, has probability

P(x | M) = q δx0 + (1 − q) P(x)   ⟹   P(M) ≤ 1q / P(x0) + (1 − q)

It is the open question underneath all of this: at best the model converges to the empirical distribution, so how much of the prior should we keep for what we have not seen?

Generalized Rejection Sampling

A chapter of the dissertation, and the seed of MPT.

I remember 2015, talking to George, my applied mathematics professor, about rejection sampling. I wondered what the distribution of the rejected samples would be, just to play. I thought that was the interesting finding. It was the rate of rejection that was interesting.

Rejection sampling is the oldest trick for drawing samples from a distribution q that you can evaluate but can’t sample from directly. You sample from a distribution p that is easy, the mother distribution, and you accept each sample with probability q(v) / (β p(v)). Whatever survives is distributed exactly as q. No burn in period, every accepted sample is clean and independent.

Theory · The acceptance rate is the probability of the model

With the smallest allowed β, the probability of accepting a sample is

acceptance = minv p(v)q(v)

Put the prior in place of p and the model in place of q, and that is the Maximum Probability bound. A model that disagrees with the prior a lot is a model you would have to reject many samples to reach.

The generalization

The catch is that to find the best β you need to scan every state first, and that costs as much as sampling the hard way. In the dissertation I showed that rejection sampling is a Monte Carlo version of the Taylor expansion of division. Once you see it that way, the condition rejection sampling needs turns out to be stricter than what the series needs.

Theory · The recursive linearization trick

Let r = q / p. For any α ≠ 0, the target satisfies a recursion, and so does 1/x:

q = Σv p(v) ( (1 − r(v)α) q + r(v)α δv )
1x = 1α + α − xα · 1x

Unroll the second and you get the Taylor series of 1/x around α. Read each step of the first as “accept and stop, or continue”, and you get rejection sampling, which needs 0 ≤ r/α ≤ 1. The series only needs

0 < E[ r(v) / α ] < 2

So samples may carry integer weights, ⌊r/α⌋ plus a coin flip for the fraction, the running coefficient may turn negative, and α can be tuned while sampling without biasing anything, since α does not change the answer. α = E[r] converges fastest. The price is variance.

Derivation · ProofThe recursion, and when it convergesShow the proof

Write q as a reweighting of p by r = q / p, with δv the point mass on v: q = Σv p(v) r(v) δv / Σv p(v) r(v). Moving terms around and adding αq to both sides, for any α ≠ 0:

Σv p(v) ((1 − r(v)/α) q + (r(v)/α) δv) = q

Unrolling it n times leaves a remainder E[1 − r/α]n q, a geometric factor. The series converges when |1 − E[r/α]| < 1, that is, when

0 < E[ r(v) / α ] < 2

Rejection sampling is the Monte Carlo version with an accept variable of probability r(v)/α, which needs 0 ≤ r(v)/α ≤ 1 for every v, a stricter condition. Since Ep[r] = 1, the remainder vanishes in one step on average when α = E[r], the fastest rate. The same algebra on x f(x) = 1 gives f(x) = 1/α + ((α − x)/α) f(x), whose unrolling is the Taylor series of 1/x around α, convergent for 0 < x/α < 2. ∎

A simulation of the generalized sampler

Here both samplers estimate the same target from the same uniform mother distribution. Each run of the generalized sampler walks the recursion: draw v, take k = ⌊r(v)/α⌋ plus a coin flip, add the current coefficient times k to v, multiply the coefficient by 1 − k, and stop when it is zero. Coefficients can go negative, so single estimates can have negative bars, but on average they are exactly right. The difference that matters: rejection sampling has to know max r before it starts, which means scanning every state. The generalized sampler never needs it.

Binary ICA: Bit Level AI

Bit level learning with invertible gates. What I am working on now. Unpublished.

There is a way to look at bitstrings, and there is hope to use our computation efficiently. Take a pile of bitstrings and find an invertible boolean function that makes the bits independent of each other.

Why bits: the way toward energy efficient AI

Why pack bits into real numbers and pay for expensive arithmetic, when NAND alone is universal?

How AI computes today

Every value is a 16 or 32 bit floating point number. The model spends its energy multiplying and adding them in real valued space, and moving them in and out of memory. A single floating point multiplier is thousands of transistors, and most of the bits it carries are noise the task never needed.

How it could compute

Any boolean function, and so any computation on bits, can be built from NAND gates alone. A gate is a handful of transistors. Build the model directly out of gates, learn which gates, and the model is the circuit: no floating point, no multiplications, a bit stored as a bit. That saves energy and memory at the root, not by compressing a real valued model afterwards.

This is why I came back to bits. Binary ICA is the learning part: it finds gates that make the bits of the data independent, and MPT keeps it from memorizing.

This was one of my dearest research paths, and I came back to it. The premise is simple. A computer stores everything as bits anyway. In the world of bits the number of models is finite, there is no uncountable infinity of real valued fits to hide in, and the curse of dimensionality shows itself very vividly. You have to face it, which I think is healthy.

Theory · Sum of marginal entropies

Write each object as n bits, B = ⟨b1, …, bn⟩. The sum of marginal entropies is never smaller than the joint entropy:

HM = Σj H(bj) ≥ H(B)

with equality exactly when the bits are independent. A bijection f never changes the joint entropy, so minimizing HM(f(B)) over bijections is lossless compression: once the bits are independent, the distribution is the product of its marginals, you can sample it bit by bit, and run f−1 to get back to the data.

Two bits, one XOR

The smallest example is already beautiful. Two bits, each a fair coin on its own, but correlated. The map f(b1, b2) = (b1, b1 ⊕ b2) is its own inverse, and it makes them exactly independent: the second bit now only says whether they agree.

ProofThe XOR makes the two bits independentShow the proof

Let both bits be fair coins, with P(b₁ ≠ b₂) = a. Then P(00) = P(11) = (1 − a)/2 and P(01) = P(10) = a/2. Under f(b₁, b₂) = (b₁, b₁ ⊕ b₂), b₁′ = b₁ is still a fair coin and b₂′ = 1 exactly when the bits differ, so P(b₂′ = 1) = a. Check every state of B′:

P(00) = ½(1 − a) = P(b₁′ = 0) P(b₂′ = 0),   P(01) = ½a = P(b₁′ = 0) P(b₂′ = 1)

and the same for the other two states, so the joint factorizes into the marginals and b₁′, b₂′ are independent. f is its own inverse, so nothing is lost: H(B′) = H(B) = 1 + H(a), and the sum of marginal entropies falls from 2 to 1 + H(a), meeting it. ∎

For n bits there are (2n)! bijections, and the data never has enough information to pick one honestly. That is the curse of dimensionality in its binary dress. I could compress, but I couldn’t generalize. The missing ingredient, I now know, was regularization, or forgetting. And the regularizer came from MPT.

A stack of invertible gates

Today the code is a stack of gates. Each one flips one bit plane where a small boolean function of its neighbourhood says so, T ← T ⊕ f(context), the same f at every pixel. The context reads other planes around the pixel and only the already decoded part of T itself, so every gate is invertible and the inverse is the stack run backwards. A gate is fitted by how much it lowers the sum of marginal entropies on the training images, and kept only if it lowers it on images it has never seen. MPT screens each condition: an event that is too rare to recur costs more information than it saves.

On CIFAR10

The first honest numbers, measured on 10,000 test images the method never saw, decoded back bit for bit to check that nothing was lost. Raw pixels cost 8 bits each. The gates bring that to 6.03 bits, and the gap between training and test is 0.003 bits. PNG still wins, at 5.87, and I say it plainly. But the gap tells me the thing finally generalizes, and that was the whole problem.

Out of Distribution Detection, Generalization, and Robustness Triangle

ICECCME 2022. With Ehsan Emad Marvasti and Ulas Bagci, at Northwestern University.

A theorem is nice, but does it survive a thousand networks trained by someone who didn’t tune them? We trained 1080 of them to find out.

The question here is the honest, practical one. When you get a new dataset, your first attempts at hyperparameters are mostly blind. So instead of reporting the one lucky run, we trained 12 layer CNNs on CIFAR10, CIFAR100 and MNIST over a whole grid of batch sizes, objectives and values of α, five times each, and looked at the whole crowd. No batch norm, no dropout, nothing else probabilistic that could interfere with the objective. Just the backbone and MPT.

We also treated the CNN as an energy based model. Then the network doesn’t only say which label, it says how plausible the input is in the first place. And that is exactly what you need to notice an image that doesn’t belong to the world the network was trained on.

Theory · MPT in an energy based model

A network f(x; θ) defines a joint distribution on inputs and labels through the prior: P(x, y | Mθ) ∝ ef(x; θ)[y] P(x, y). The normalizer cancels and the MPT intersection objective becomes

ln ( 1N Σi ef(xi)[yi] P(xi, yi) ) − 1α ln Σx, y eαf(x)[y]

The second term suppresses the probability of the most probable states. To spot an unfamiliar input we used the max energy score, S(x) = maxy f(x; θ)[y]: the lower it is, the less the input belongs.

What the crowd of networks said

As α grows, the average test accuracy goes up and the spread goes down. On all three datasets. That second part is what I care about most: regularization here is not a trick to win a benchmark, it makes training less of a lottery.

With MPT and the energy based training, max energy reached an AUROC of 0.866 with a plain 12 layer CNN, close to methods that use 40 layer WideResNets. It is not state of the art and I don’t pretend it is. The point is the triangle: out of distribution detection, generalization and robustness moved together, with one term in the objective.

Finite State Networks

Rediscovering Deep Neural Networks Through Finite State Distributions. IEEE Access. With Ehsan Emad Marvasti, Ulas Bagci and Hassan Foroosh.

Number 4.2432 is not a point, it’s a region, because we missed the infinite zeros. In calculus class every teacher shows a point on the whiteboard with a region as thick as the marker.

This line of work started from discreteness. Not thresholding values, but reading every value inside a neural network as an unnormalized log probability over a finite number of states. A pixel is not a real number, it is a distribution over a few possible values. A feature map is a stack of distributions. And the question becomes: if every tensor in the network is a distribution, what is the natural way to compute with them?

Theory · A layer is a Bayesian classifier

The input x and each filter Fi are distributions on a finite set. The log likelihood of filter i is the Kullback Leibler divergence, the asymptotic form of the log of a multinomial, and Bayes’ rule normalizes:

P(i | x) ∝ exp( −KL(Fi ‖ x) + ln πi )

The output is again a distribution, so classifiers compose. The KL is the linear part, a convolution when the filters slide over the image. The normalization is the non linear part.

The part that surprised me: the non linearities we all use fall out by themselves. Normalize in log space and you get something that is a rotated ReLU, we called it LNorm. Normalize in probability space and you get a rotated Sigmoid, ExpNorm. Even pooling has a meaning, marginalizing over the spatial position approximates max pooling, and ReLU pairs with max pooling while Sigmoid pairs with average pooling, depending on which direction of the KL divergence you use.

We converted known architectures, Quick CIFAR, Network in Network and VGG, into their finite state versions and trained them without dropout, batch norm or preprocessing. They perform comparably to the CNNs, and stripped of those extras some even do better. And because every tensor is a distribution, you can measure things you normally can’t, like the entropy of every filter while the network learns.

In hindsight I don’t fully approve of every choice in this paper. But it was the valuable starting point. It is how I got out of the curse of dimensionality that already lives inside a single real valued scalar, and it is why I now prefer exponentially large but finite state spaces.

Dissertation

Maximum Probability Framework and Digital Probabilistic Models. University of Central Florida, 2021. Advised by Hassan Foroosh.

The dissertation is where the pieces met: a way to say what a model is and how probable it is, and a way to build networks out of distributions on finite states.

I started the PhD in 2014 at UCF, at the Computational Imaging Lab, after my bachelor’s in computer engineering at Sharif. I didn’t choose to study AI, it just grew on me. The dissertation has three parts. The Maximum Probability Theorem and the framework around it. Finite Konvolutional Networks, the digital, probabilistic counterpart of CNNs. And generalized rejection sampling, the tool for sampling from the conditional distributions these models need, which is also where MPT was born.

What I still believe

That generalization deserves rigor before we deploy AI so fast. Not approximate bounds, but the best guarantee we could possibly have. That regularization should follow from how a model behaves, not from the coordinates we wrote it in. And that there is still a way to look at bitstrings, and hope to use our computation efficiently. The dissertation ends with finite state models as the backbone for that. I am still on it, see .

Applied ML

Vehicular Perception

Cooperative perception between vehicles, and traffic vision on real roads.

A theory of models is only worth something if it still holds on a highway at night, in the rain, with a truck in the way.

Cars that see for each other

As a coauthor with Ehsan Emad Marvasti, Yaser P. Fallah and colleagues, I worked on cooperative perception for connected vehicles. A car’s LIDAR cannot see through the truck in front of it, but the car on the other side of the truck can. The question is what they should share. Raw point clouds are too heavy for a wireless link, and final detections throw away what the other car half saw. Our answer was in between: share the features inside the detector network, align them, fuse them, and produce one detection from both points of view. A second paper made the sharing adapt to the link budget, sending less when the channel is narrow.

Highway Eyes and Parkie

From 2022 to 2024 I was the founding engineer and principal AI scientist at Currus AI, and built Visioncube, the perception backend behind two products on real roads: Highway Eyes for the Central Florida Expressway Authority, and Parkie for the City of Winter Park. Camera streams come in, vehicles and people are detected and tracked, each camera is placed on the ground with a homography and GPS, and the tracks become counts, speeds, congestion, parking occupancy and alerts for crashes, stalled cars, objects on the road and wrong way drivers.

The research side showed up where it always does, at the edges of the training data. When lighting and weather left the scenes the detector was trained on, it was updated online, with a probabilistic uncertainty on each update, so the system could tell when it was outside its world. That is the out of distribution question again, this time with traffic in it.

Geography of Opinions

A geographic social network. Organic, suggestion free, AI free. Since October 2024.

After years of research in AI, I built a social network with no AI in it at all.

We have all this technology and still no public platform, with public stakes, to organize ourselves politically and geographically. Geography of Opinions is that attempt: people vote and state opinions on a map, and how information flows through the network stays in their hands. There is no recommendation system deciding what you see, no model ranking your neighbours for you.

It comes from the same place as the research. Technology reduces the continuum of reality to a finite set of letters, whether in a science book or bits in a computer. Used well, that reduction serves the part of our rational mind that wants to organize, and here it serves how we organize ourselves on the ground, with 8 billion people and a phone in every hand.

Artament

A platform for art institutions and their artists. Coming soon.

Every artist gets an identity for life, every artwork a permanent identity, and the institutions that know the work vouch for it.

Artament gives an art institution, a foundation, a museum or a gallery, a public site it designs itself and a dashboard to run its people, events and verifications. Each artist gets an Artist ID and a studio of their own. Every artwork becomes an art item with a permanent Art ID. Institutions verify that a work is original and that its details are right, and once it is verified the artist sets its price.

Every page on Artament is data, its words and its look, so one platform can serve many institutions, and the people who own a page design it. This website has a twin there: Qualia In Dualia is one of its artist studios.
