Research

I failed my statistics course in undergrad, and somehow ended up deep in probability theory and information theory. I didn’t choose AI, it grew on me. What kept me there was the question of unknownness: what a model is, how probable it is, and how little we can get away with assuming.

Every idea here is connected to one theorem. It says a model has a probability, and that making a model more probable removes information from it. Forgetting, as it turns out, is regularization.

Start with the theorem The story of the research

The story of the research

From a pile of bits to a theorem about forgetting, and back

  1. IBitsI wanted invertible boolean functions that make bits independent. I could compress, but I couldn’t generalize.
  2. IIFinite statesSo I rebuilt networks from distributions on finite states. The hidden distributions kept collapsing, and the optimization stopped.
  3. IIIA rejection rateAn old report on rejection sampling had the answer: one minus the rejection rate is the probability of the model.
  4. IVThe theoremModels are events with a probability. Making them more probable strips their information content. Forgetting is regularization, for any model.
  5. V1080 networksAcross 1080 blindly tuned networks, MPT raised the mean generalization and shrank its variance. Consistently.
  6. VIBack to bitsNow the missing ingredient is in place, and the bits generalize.

The longer, more personal version, with the cigarettes on the staircase, is the Research Bio.
